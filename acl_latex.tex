\begin{abstract}
We introduce AuthBench, a large-scale multilingual benchmark for Authorship Representation, enabling evaluation of authorship Attribution and Verification across numerous genres and lengths. AuthBench contains 293,029 documents of diverse lengths (short, medium, long, and extra-long), authored by 53,281 individuals across ten widely used languages (en, zh, hi, es, fr, ar, ru, de, ja, ko), spanning 9 primary genres with 62 fine-grained subgenres. AuthBench supports two complementary evaluations: (i) \emph{authorship attribution}, operationalized as same-author document retrieval, scored by Success@K, Recall@K, and nDCG@K; and (ii) \emph{authorship verification}, i.e.,  same-author binary decisions over query--candidate pairs, scored by equal error rate (EER). We comprehensively evaluate state-of-the-art (SOTA) instruction-tuned and embedding models on AuthBench. Experiments with SOTA models show that performance remains far from saturated: the best Success@5 reaches only 0.542, and the best overall EER is 0.078. Moreover, SOTA models exhibit substantial performance variation across languages, document lengths, and genres, highlighting persistent challenges for robust authorship modeling. We release AuthBench along with its evaluation toolkit anonymously at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/AURA_Bench-366E/README.md}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Authorship representation \cite{huang2025authorship,habib2025trends,tyo2022state} examines whether a model can encode author-specific linguistic and stylistic features into reusable text representations, which is an important capability of current state-of-the-art (SOTA) models. This ability has a wide range of applications, including cybersecurity and digital forensics \citep{abbasi2008writeprints,narayanan2012feasibility,kumar2017army,caliskan2015deanonymizing}, and underpins additional tasks such as plagiarism detection \citep{potthast2014panplagiarism,barron-cedeno-etal-2013-plagiarism} and machine-generated text detection \citep{przystalski2025stylometry,aityan2025lightweight,alshaibani2026arabic,martinek2024deepfakeads}.

Driven by significant progress in large language models (LLMs) \cite{yang2025qwen3,zhang2025qwen3,grattafiori2024llama}, SOTA models have become increasingly generalizable across downstream tasks, languages, and varying input lengths. However, existing benchmarks fall short of comprehensively evaluating these capabilities in authorship representation, as they are typically restricted to single languages or genres. For instance, widely used datasets like the Blog Authorship Corpus \citep{schler2006effects}, Enron emails \citep{klimt2004enron}, and PAN\footnote{\rev{Many PAN editions focus on a single genre or discourse setting, although some tasks explicitly consider cross-discourse conditions (e.g., PAN 2022).}} shared tasks \citep{potthast2016whowrote,bevendorff2020pan} are limited to specific domains. Similarly, other benchmarks focusing on scientific writing \citep{bevendorff2023smauc}, cross-domain settings (e.g., LUAR) \citep{riverasoto2021luar}, or journalist-centric pairings \citep{ma2025crossnews} remain narrowly scoped. Furthermore, data distributions in these benchmarks are often highly skewed: for example, scientific authorship datasets predominantly feature documents exceeding 500 words. Finally, current benchmarks lack a unified framework to support both \emph{retrieval-style authorship representation} to find documents by the same author in a collection of documents and \emph{verification-style authorship attribution} to confirm that a given pair of documents is by the same author.

To overcome the above limitations of existing benchmarks on authorship representation, we introduce AuthBench, a large-scale multilingual benchmark for Authorship Representation enabling Attribution and Verification across genres and document lengths. AuthBench is constructed from 13 public data sources (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-raw-sources} for details). We normalize all data into a unified format,  filter out low-quality samples, and perform stratified sampling to balance genre and length distributions across languages. Finally, we conduct additional automated checks and manual inspections to ensure the overall quality of AuthBench. As a result, AuthBench comprises 293,029 documents of diverse lengths (short, medium, long, and extra-long), authored by 53,281 individuals across ten widely used languages (en, zh, hi, es, fr, ar, ru, de, ja, ko), spanning nine primary genres with 62 fine-grained subgenres. AuthBench supports two complementary evaluation tasks: (i) \emph{authorship attribution}, formulated as a retrieval task evaluated with ranking-based metrics (Success@K, Recall@K, nDCG@K); and (ii) \emph{authorship verification}, formulated as binary decision over query–candidate pairs and evaluated using threshold-based metrics (EER). We release standardized dataset splits and a unified evaluation toolkit to facilitate reproducible and comprehensive evaluation. 

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the SOTA models on AuthBench, including embedding, instructed, and base LLMs. The evaluation results show that the best overall model performance of Success@5 on authorship attribution is 0.542, and the best overall model performance of EER on authorship attribution is 0.078. Moreover, SOTA models exhibit substantial performance variation across languages, document lengths, and genres, highlighting persistent challenges for robust authorship modeling.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\textbf{Authorship representation.} 
Authorship representation \cite{huang2025authorship,habib2025trends,tyo2022state} seeks to identify persistent author-specific signals in written documents. The field is commonly structured around two core evaluation tasks: (i) authorship attribution, which links a document to its author (often framed as closed-set classification or large-scale retrieval), and (ii) authorship verification, which determines whether two documents were written by the same author. In parallel, authorship representation learning develops document embeddings that encode stylistic signals and can be used to support both attribution and verification at scale. Recent works, including LUAR \citep{riverasoto2021luar}, style-aware embedding methods \citep{wang2023canrep}, and stylistic disentanglement analyses \citep{michailidis2022stylometric}, operationalize attribution via embedding similarity and retrieval-style evaluation (e.g., Success@K, Recall@K, nDCG@K), and evaluate verification using pairwise decision metrics such as equal error rate (EER).

\textbf{Authorship Benchmark Development.}
Early authorship benchmarks were largely built on small-scale, monolingual, and domain-specific corpora, as summarized in foundational surveys \citep{stamatatos2009survey, neal2017surveying}. Datasets such as the Blog Authorship Corpus \citep{schler2006effects}, the Enron email dataset \citep{klimt2004enron}, and later English-centric collections used in neural modeling studies \citep{ruder2016character, jafariakinabad2019styleaware} supported important methodological progress, but provided limited coverage in terms of language diversity, genre breadth, and document length control.
Besides, none of them provides the standard evaluation protocols, making it hard to evaluate current SOTA models. The PAN shared tasks subsequently established standardized evaluation protocols for authorship-related problems \citep{bevendorff2020pan}. While influential, individual PAN editions typically addressed a single task formulation and relied on datasets restricted to specific languages and genres. More recent work has sought to broaden authorship evaluation beyond these settings by introducing more domain-oriented authorship corpora, such as scientific writing datasets (SMAuC) \citep{bevendorff2023smauc} and journalist news–tweet pairings (CROSSNEWS) \citep{ma2025crossnews}. In parallel, LLM-focused authorship benchmarks (e.g., AIDBench) \citep{wen2024aidbench} and studies examining authorship risks in large language models \citep{huang2024aa_llm, huang2024authorship} have emerged to evaluate the flaws of the SOTA models on authorship. Nevertheless, despite this growing body of work, existing benchmarks remain predominantly domain-specific, often exhibit highly imbalanced author distributions, and lack unified control over language, genre, and document length. We provide a comparison of our Authbench with existing benchmarks in Table~\ref{tab:bench-comparison}.

\subsection{Benchmark Construction}
\label{sec:construction}
Figure~\ref{fig:processing_flow} illustrates the construction workflow of AuthBench. We constructed the benchmark using 13 publicly available data sources. Descriptions of these datasets are available in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-raw-sources}. To ensure consistency across heterogeneous corpora, we first normalize each source into a unified record format containing the following fields: language, genre, source ID, anonymized author ID, document content, and token length. We provide examples in Figure \ref{fig:json-examples}. We then remove redundancy using a combination of exact hashing and near-duplicate detection. To further improve data quality, we apply safety and quality filters to exclude low-quality or problematic documents. Next, to achieve balanced distributions, we perform bucket-based sampling across languages, document length ranges, and genres, while enforcing per-author caps to prevent dominance by highly prolific writers. Finally, to produce leakage-resistant data splits, we apply stratified partitioning with deduplication-aware assignment, ensuring that duplicate or near-duplicate documents do not appear across different splits. We additionally conduct automated checks and manual inspections to validate the quality of the final AuthBench. Automated checks verify schema integrity, bucket assignment, and split hygiene (no author overlap and no duplicate or near-duplicate leakage across splits). For manual inspection, the authors randomly sample documents across languages, genres, and length buckets to screen for noise, boilerplate, corrupted text, and unsafe content. Complete construction details are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-construction-details}.

\subsection{Statistics of AuthBench}
\label{sec:statbench}

After construction, AuthBench comprises 293{,}029 documents authored by 53{,}281 individuals across 10 languages: English (en), Spanish (es), Chinese (zh), French (fr), German (de), Arabic (ar), Russian (ru), Japanese (ja), Korean (ko), and Hindi (hi). AuthBench spans 9 primary genres, including \texttt{social\_media}, \texttt{news}, \texttt{blog}, \texttt{literature}, \texttt{media\_reviews}, \texttt{ecommerce\_reviews}, \texttt{poetry}, \texttt{research\_paper}, and \rev{\texttt{qna}}. Documents in AuthBench are further categorized into four token-length buckets: short (1–10 tokens), medium (11–100), long (101–500), and extra-long ($>$500). \section{Experiment Setup}
\textbf{Evaluation details.}  We embed queries and candidates with each model’s encoder (or the final hidden states of LLM backbones) using a unified pipeline: tokenize each input, extract the last hidden states, pool into a single vector (mean pooling by default; alternatives such as CLS/last are supported where appropriate), and L2-normalize the resulting embedding. Similarities are computed with the cosine distance between normalized embeddings. We use batch size 32 and the full-sized within-split candidate pool; verification EER uses sampled 50 negatives per query as defined in Appendix~\ref{subsec:eval-protocol}.

\noindent\textbf{Models.} We evaluate a range of SOTA base, embedding, and instruction-tuned models. Full model details are provided in Table~\ref{tab:models-evaluated} of Appendix~\ref{subsec:eval-protocol}.

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.} AuthBench supports both authorship attribution and authorship verification tasks. 
For authorship attribution, we report Success@5 (S@5), Recall@5 (R@5), and nDCG@5. 
For authorship verification, we report EER. 
Formal definitions of all evaluation metrics are provided in Appendix~\ref{subsec:eval-protocol}.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:mainresults}
\textbf{Finding 1: base LLMs consistently provide the strongest authorship signals, outperforming instruction-tuned and embedding-based models.} 
Table~\ref{tab:overall-leaderboard} summarizes the overall performance of a subset of SOTA models on AuthBench. Due to space constraints, we report results for all evaluated models in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-full-results}. We categorize the evaluated models into four groups: instruction-tuned LLMs, base LLMs, instruction-tuned embedding models, and base embedding models.

Overall, base LLMs consistently outperform all other model groups, often surpassing their instruction-tuned counterparts. For example, \texttt{llama3-8b} achieves the best overall authorship attribution performance, with scores of 0.542 (S@5), 0.537 (R@5), and 0.441 (nDCG@5), outperforming \texttt{llama3-8b-instruct} across all metrics. However, model scale is not the sole determinant of performance. Notably, the compact \texttt{qwen2.5-3b} outperforms the significantly larger \texttt{deepseek-llm-7b-base} (S@5: 0.496 vs. 0.479), highlighting the efficiency of recent architectures in capturing stylistic features despite limited parameter counts.

While base LLMs dominate attribution metrics, instruction-tuned LLMs remain competitive in the verification task. Remarkably, \texttt{qwen2.5-3b-instruct} achieves the lowest EER of 0.078 across all evaluated models. This suggests that while instruction tuning might degrade attribution performance (e.g., S@5), it may preserve or even enhance the model's decision boundaries for binary authorship verification (i.e., EER).

In contrast, instruction-tuned embedding models exhibit noticeably weaker attribution performance than base LLMs. Finally, base embedding models perform the worst overall due to the small model size. Strikingly, traditional dense retrievers often struggle to capture stylistic nuances beyond lexical overlap. As shown in the table, several base embedding models (e.g., \texttt{allenai-specter}) perform comparably to---or even worse than---the simple \texttt{TF-IDF} baseline (S@5: 0.326), further underscoring the necessity of LLM-based models for authorship representation.

\subsection{Performance by Length}
\textbf{Within each model scale, large models perform better on \texttt{medium} length documents, whereas small models perform better on \texttt{short} documents. Moreover, when comparing across scales, small models achieve comparable or even superior performance to large models on \texttt{short} documents.} Table~\ref{tab:length-s10-top} presents a fine-grained breakdown of model performance across four document lengths. Due to space constraints, we report results for all evaluated models and additional metrics in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-full-results}.
Performance for nearly all LLM-based models peaks in the \texttt{medium} length category. 
For instance, \texttt{llama3.1-8b} jumps from 0.407 on \texttt{short} documents to 0.625 on \texttt{medium} documents, before declining on longer inputs. This suggests that medium-length documents provide an optimal balance: they contain sufficient stylistic markers (unlike \texttt{short} texts) without introducing the noise or context dilution often found in \texttt{long} or \texttt{extra\_long} documents. Interestingly, small models achieve competitive performance on \texttt{short} documents compared to significantly larger LLMs. For example, \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} achieves a score of 0.444 with only 109M parameters, rivaling the 8B-parameter \texttt{llama3.1-8b-instruct} (0.481). This suggests that in short document regimes, authorship attribution relies less on complex semantic modeling and more on local stylistic features or lexical patterns, which compact embedding models can extract highly efficiently.


Base LLMs demonstrate superior stability as text length increases compared to embedding models. On \texttt{extra\_long} texts, \texttt{llama3-8b-instruct} achieves the highest score of 0.427, maintaining strong performance where others falter. In sharp contrast, the lexical \texttt{TF-IDF} baseline collapses on longer texts, dropping from 0.444 (\texttt{short}) to a dismal 0.227 (\texttt{extra\_long}). This confirms that while simple lexical statistics suffice for short documents, deep contextual understanding is a prerequisite for modeling the coherent stylistic patterns of lengthy documents.

Most notably, the fine-tuned \texttt{multilingual-e5-large} establishes a new SOTA on the leaderboard, surpassing the previous best model (\texttt{llama3.1-8b}) by roughly 10 points in S@5 and 0.04 in EER. These results indicate that while current SOTA models are powerful generalized feature extractors, they still lack adaptation for specific downstream tasks like authorship attribution. AuthBench serves as an effective resource for bridging this gap through post-training.

\section{Conclusion}
In this work, we introduced \textbf{AuthBench}, a comprehensive benchmark designed to standardize the evaluation of authorship representation across diverse languages, genres, and document lengths. Our extensive evaluation demonstrates that while base LLMs currently provide the strongest stylistic signals—significantly outperforming traditional embedding models in complex scenarios—the task remains far from solved. Significant performance gaps persist, particularly in short context attribution and cross-genre generalization. Furthermore, our training experiments confirm that AuthBench serves as an effective substrate for domain adaptation, enabling models to better capture subtle authorship cues. We hope this resource facilitates future research into more robust, efficient, and universally adaptable authorship analysis.

\section*{Limitations} A primary limitation of this work is the uneven distribution of document lengths and genres across languages, which stems from the inherent characteristics of the public corpora utilized. We aim to address this distributional imbalance in future iterations by curating more balanced sources. Additionally, due to the large scale of AuthBench, manual verification was feasible only for a representative subset of the data. However, the robust training dynamics and performance gains demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} serve as strong empirical evidence of the dataset's high quality and effective labeling.
In future work, we plan to further inspect all the examples in the benchmark.

\section{AuthBench Construction Details}
\label{sec:appendix-construction-details}

This appendix provides the full construction specification for AuthBench, including normalization, deduplication, safety and quality filtering heuristics, bucketed sampling, and split construction.
Section~\ref{sec:construction} in the main paper contains a concise summary.

AuthBench is constructed via a unified, reproducible pipeline that transforms heterogeneous raw corpora into a balanced and quality-controlled benchmark. The pipeline enforces (i) schema consistency across sources, (ii) strict de-duplication to reduce leakage and redundancy, (iii) conservative safety and quality filtering, and (iv) stratified split construction that preserves distributional balance across key axes.

\subsection{Source Ingestion and Normalization}
\label{subsec:construct-ingest}
Let $\mathcal{S}=\{S_1,\dots,S_m\}$ denote the set of raw sources (platforms, domains, and languages). Each source yields a multiset of candidate items
$\mathcal{D}_i=\{d_{i,1},\dots,d_{i,n_i}\}$ with raw text and source-specific metadata.
We standardize all items into a unified record
\[
x = (\texttt{id},\ \ell,\ g,\ \sigma,\ \tau,\ t,\ \mathbf{y}),
\]
where $\ell$ is language, $g$ is (macro-)genre/domain, $\sigma$ is the source identifier, $\tau$ is the task type (if applicable), $t$ is the text field used for retrieval/attribution, and $\mathbf{y}$ denotes optional structured labels (e.g., class label, reference answer).

We apply deterministic text normalization $N(\cdot)$ to each raw text string:
(i) Unicode canonicalization to NFC, (ii) removal of control characters, and (iii) whitespace canonicalization (collapse repeated whitespace; trim leading/trailing spaces). We tokenize with a fixed tokenizer $T(\cdot)$ used throughout the benchmark and compute token length
\[
L(t) \;=\; \lvert T(t)\rvert .
\]
We retain minimal provenance metadata (language, source, genre/domain, task type) and store $L(t)$ for downstream filtering and bucketing.

\subsection{De-duplication and Near-Duplicate Removal}
\label{subsec:construct-dedup}
To reduce redundancy and prevent train--test contamination via repeated content, we perform exact and near-duplicate removal within the candidate pool prior to splitting.

\paragraph{Exact duplicates.}
For each item with normalized text $t$, we compute a stable hash $h(t)$ (e.g., SHA-256) and drop duplicates by keeping a single representative per unique hash:
\[
\mathcal{D}^{\text{exact}} \;=\; \{x \in \cup_i \mathcal{D}_i \;:\; h(t)\ \text{is unique}\}.
\]

\paragraph{Near duplicates.}
We represent each text $t$ using character $n$-grams (we use $n=5$) and apply a locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) scheme (e.g., MinHash) to approximate Jaccard similarity between texts. For two texts $t$ and $t'$, let $G(t)$ be the multiset of 5-grams and define
\[
J(t,t') \;=\; \frac{\lvert G(t)\cap G(t')\rvert}{\lvert G(t)\cup G(t')\rvert}.
\]
Given a threshold $\theta_{\text{nd}}$, we remove $x$ if there exists a previously accepted item $x'$ such that $J(t,t') \ge \theta_{\text{nd}}$.
We additionally remove common boilerplate patterns via deterministic rules (e.g., copyright headers, navigation lists) before similarity computation to avoid spurious matches.

\subsection{Safety and Quality Filtering}
\label{subsec:construct-filter}
We apply conservative safety and quality filters to exclude unsafe, malformed, or low-information items. An item is retained if it passes all filters below.

\paragraph{Policy and toxicity.}
Let $s_{\text{tox}}(t)\in[0,1]$ denote a toxicity/policy risk score produced by one or more classifiers. We drop items above a conservative threshold $\tau_{\text{tox}}$:
\[
s_{\text{tox}}(t) \;>\; \tau_{\text{tox}} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{drop}.
\]

\paragraph{Language identification and script consistency.}
Let $\hat{\ell}(t)$ be the predicted language (optional) and let $S_{\ell}$ be the expected Unicode script set for language $\ell$ (e.g., Latin for \texttt{en/es/fr/de}, Cyrillic for \texttt{ru}, Arabic for \texttt{ar}, CJK scripts for \texttt{zh/ja/ko}).
Define the script-match ratio over letters:
\[
r_{\text{script}}(t;\ell) \;=\;
\frac{\#\{\text{letters in } t \text{ whose script}\in S_{\ell}\}}
{\#\{\text{letters in } t\}},
\]
evaluated when $t$ contains at least $8$ letters. We drop if $r_{\text{script}}(t;\ell) < \tau_{\text{script}}(\ell)$. If $r_{\text{script}}$ is low/undefined and language identification is enabled, we additionally drop when $\hat{\ell}(t)$ is incompatible with the target language $\ell$.

\paragraph{Length bounds.}
We enforce per-task token limits to avoid trivially short or excessively long instances. For each task type $\tau$, we specify bounds $(L_{\min}(\tau), L_{\max}(\tau))$ and drop if
\[
L(t) < L_{\min}(\tau) \quad \text{or} \quad L(t) > L_{\max}(\tau).
\]
We also discard empty items where $L(t)=0$.

\paragraph{Low-information and formatting heuristics.}
We compute a set of interpretable text-quality statistics and filter via thresholds. Let $w=T(t)$ be the token sequence.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unique token ratio \rev{(type-token ratio over whitespace tokens)}:}
  \[
  r_{\text{uniq}}(t) \;=\; \frac{\lvert \text{unique}(w)\rvert}{\lvert w\rvert},
  \]
  drop if $r_{\text{uniq}}(t) < \tau_{\text{uniq}}$.

  \item \textbf{Symbol ratio:} let $\#\text{sym}(t)$ count non-letter and non-digit characters (whitespace handling is source-dependent). Define
  \[
  r_{\text{sym}}(t) \;=\; \frac{\#\text{sym}(t)}{\lvert t\rvert_{\text{chars}}},
  \]
  drop if $r_{\text{sym}}(t) > \tau_{\text{sym}}$.

  \item \textbf{Maximum repeated-character run:} let $m_{\text{rep}}(t)$ be the maximum run length of identical non-space characters. Drop if $m_{\text{rep}}(t) > K_{\text{rep}}$.

  \item \textbf{Alphabetic character ratio:}
  \[
  r_{\alpha}(t) \;=\; \frac{\#\text{alphabetic chars}}{\#\text{non-space chars}},
  \]
  drop if $r_{\alpha}(t) < \tau_{\alpha}$.

    \item \textbf{Alphabetic token ratio:}
    let $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ denote the indicator function, and define
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
    \label{eq:alpha-token-ratio}
    r_{\text{tok}}(t)
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{\lvert w\rvert}
    \sum_{j=1}^{\lvert w\rvert}
    \mathbb{I}\!\left[\alpha(w_j)\right],
    \end{aligned}
    \]
    where $\alpha(w_j)$ is true if token $w_j$ contains at least one alphabetic character.
    We drop an item if $r_{\text{tok}}(t) < \tau_{\text{tok}}$.


    \item \textbf{Single-letter density:}
    let $u_j$ be true when $w_j$ is a single-letter token. Define
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
    r_{1}(t)
    &=\frac{1}{\lvert w\rvert}
    \sum_{j=1}^{\lvert w\rvert}\mathbb{I}[u_j], \\
    m_{1}(t)
    &=\max \{ \\
    &\quad \text{length of any consecutive run of } u_j
    \}.
    \end{aligned}
    \]
    We drop an item if $r_{1}(t) > \tau_{1}$ or $m_{1}(t) \ge K_{1}$.


\end{itemize}
For public-domain style corpora where heavy punctuation is common, we optionally include a maximum consecutive-symbol constraint $m_{\text{sym}}(t) \le K_{\text{sym}}$.

\subsection{Task-Specific Structuring and Validation}
\label{subsec:construct-struct}
Some sources provide structured fields (e.g., question--answer pairs, labels, prompts, references). For each task type $\tau$, we define a schema $\mathcal{F}_{\tau}$ (required fields) and retain an instance only if all required fields are present and non-empty after normalization:
\[
\forall f \in \mathcal{F}_{\tau},\;\; \texttt{field}_f \neq \varnothing.
\]
Malformed entries are discarded. For long documents exceeding task-specific caps, we segment into contiguous chunks that preserve local coherence; each chunk inherits provenance metadata and receives a unique \texttt{id} derived from the original record and chunk offset.

\subsection{Balanced Bucketed Sampling}
\label{subsec:construct-buckets}
The filtered pool can be imbalanced across languages, lengths, and domains. \rev{We therefore construct the final benchmark via hierarchical bucketed sampling over salient axes.}

Let each item $x$ be assigned a bucket key
\[
{\color{red} b(x) \;=\; (\ell,\ g,\ k),}
\]
\rev{where $\ell$ is language, $g$ is genre/domain, and $k$ is a token-length bin derived from $L(t)$. In the implementation, length bins are the fixed ranges \texttt{short}, \texttt{medium}, \texttt{long}, and \texttt{extra\_long}.} For each bucket $b$, let $N_b$ be its available count.

\rev{We specify target proportions (or quotas) $Q_b$ that enforce: (i) approximately balanced mass across languages, (ii) target genre proportions within each language, and (iii) target length-bucket proportions within each selected language--genre slice.} We then sample without replacement within each bucket:
\[
\mathcal{B} \;=\; \bigcup_{b} \textsc{Sample}\big(\{x: b(x)=b\},\ Q_b\big).
\]
\rev{If some buckets are underfull ($N_b < Q_b$), we redistribute the deficit to leftover items from the same language, first within the targeted genre and then via a spill pool, while preserving the overall language target as closely as possible.}

\rev{To keep the earlier build stage memory-bounded, the implementation uses bounded shuffling of dataset streams and a per-author reservoir before the final post-filtering, deduplication, and balanced sampling stages.}

\subsection{Post-processing and Final De-duplication}
\label{subsec:construct-post}
\rev{We apply a final normalization pass to collapse letter-by-letter spacing artifacts, re-tokenize each document, recompute its length bucket, and re-check quality constraints after normalization.}

\rev{We then perform exact-text and near-text de-duplication, followed by an optional cross-source author-profile de-duplication step to reduce residual redundancy and leakage.}

\subsection{Split Construction}
\label{subsec:construct-splits}
\rev{We construct train/dev/test splits via stratification over the same bucket space to preserve balance. In the implementation, documents are first grouped by language and then bucketed by $(g,k)$, where $g$ is genre/domain and $k$ is the length bucket. Within each bucket, documents are deterministically shuffled with a fixed random seed and divided according to the target split ratios, with integer split counts allocated per bucket.}
We enforce disjointness by design: items with identical or near-duplicate content are never placed in different splits, and when sources require provenance isolation, we additionally enforce source-level disjointness (no cross-split collisions within the specified unit, e.g., document or thread).

Overall, this pipeline yields a diverse benchmark with controlled coverage over language and length, reduced redundancy, conservative safety guarantees, and splits that preserve stratified balance for reliable evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval-protocol}
For retrieval, each test document is used as a query and ranked against a candidate pool drawn from the same split.
For verification, we evaluate on labeled query--candidate pairs derived from the same within-split pools, reporting EER.
All results are computed without task-specific fine-tuning to ensure comparability across model families.
We report both aggregate performance and mandatory stratified breakdowns by language, genre, and length.
Length buckets follow the benchmark definition: short (1--10 tokens), medium (11--100), long (101--500), and extra\_long (>500).

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}
Let $q$ be a query with candidate set $C_q$ and positives $P_q \subset C_q$.
Let $\pi_q$ be the ranking of candidates by similarity. We define the binary
relevance at rank $i$ as
\[
\mathrm{rel}_i =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \pi_q(i) \in P_q, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
For $K=10$, we compute:

\[
\mathrm{Recall@}K(q) = \frac{1}{|P_q|} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathrm{rel}_i
\]
\[
\mathrm{DCG@}K(q) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \frac{\mathrm{rel}_i}{\log_2(i+1)}
\]
\[
\mathrm{IDCG@}K(q) = \sum_{i=1}^{\min(K,|P_q|)} \frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)},
\]
\[
\mathrm{nDCG@}K(q) = \frac{\mathrm{DCG@}K(q)}{\mathrm{IDCG@}K(q)}
\]

We define Success@K as:
\[
\mathrm{Success@}K(q) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathrm{rel}_i > 0, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
We report $S@5$, $R@5$, and nDCG@5 as macro-averages over queries.

For authorship verification, each query--candidate pair is assigned a similarity
score $s \in \mathbb{R}$ and classified using a threshold $\tau$.
Let $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ denote the sets of positive and negative pairs,
respectively. The false acceptance rate (FAR) and false rejection rate (FRR) at
threshold $\tau$ are defined as
\begin{align}
\mathrm{FAR}(\tau) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|} \sum_{(q,c)\in\mathcal{N}} \mathbb{I}[s(q,c) \ge \tau], \\
\mathrm{FRR}(\tau) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(q,c)\in\mathcal{P}} \mathbb{I}[s(q,c) < \tau].
\end{align}
The Equal Error Rate (EER) is defined as the operating point where the two error
rates are equal:
\[
\mathrm{EER} = \mathrm{FAR}(\tau^\ast) = \mathrm{FRR}(\tau^\ast),
\]
\[
\text{where } \tau^\ast = \arg\min_{\tau} \left| \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) - \mathrm{FRR}(\tau) \right|.
\]

\section{Responsible NLP Checklist Details}
\label{sec:checklist}

This appendix provides checklist-specific details for ACL submission items B4, B6, C2, C3, D1, D3, D4, and E1.
Where applicable, we point to the primary sections in the main paper and provide additional operational details here.

\subsection{Personally Identifying Information (PII) or Offensive Content}
\label{sec:checklist-b4}
\paragraph{PII.}
AuthBench is constructed exclusively from publicly available datasets with established licenses and distribution practices (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-raw-sources}).
We do not release raw author identifiers. Instead, we anonymize author identifiers by hashing (Section~\ref{sec:overview}; Section~\ref{sec:construction}).
We also retain only minimal provenance metadata needed for analysis and reproducibility (language, genre, source, token length), and we do not include user profile fields, URLs, or platform-specific identifiers beyond the hashed author ID.

\paragraph{Offensive or unsafe content.}
We apply conservative safety filtering during construction (Section~\ref{sec:construction}; Appendix~\ref{subsec:construct-filter}).
Specifically, we filter items using policy/toxicity classifiers (dropping items above a conservative threshold), enforce language and script consistency checks, and remove malformed or low-information items using interpretable heuristics (Appendix~\ref{subsec:construct-filter}).
We additionally deduplicate and near-deduplicate content to reduce leakage and repeated unsafe fragments (Appendix~\ref{subsec:construct-dedup}).

\paragraph{Residual risk.}
Because sources originate from the open web and large public corpora, residual PII or offensive text may persist despite filtering and deduplication.
We mitigate this risk through anonymization, conservative filtering, provenance tracking, and explicit documentation of prohibited uses.

We report (i) aggregate metrics and (ii) mandatory stratified breakdowns by language, genre, and length (Section~\ref{sec:appendix-full-results}).

\subsection{Topic-Leakage Controls and Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:topic-leakage}
A common concern in authorship evaluation is that systems exploit topical overlap or lexical duplication rather than author-specific signals.
To make such shortcut behavior explicit, we include a sparse TF-IDF cosine baseline and stress-test it by modifying candidate pool construction while keeping evaluation strictly within split.
Concretely, in addition to the default within-split candidate pool, we construct a topic-selected variant where candidates are restricted to match a coarse topic label (when available from source metadata or derived topic tags).
Table~\ref{tab:topic-leakage-tfidf} shows two complementary effects: (i) TF-IDF improves under topic selection (higher S@5), indicating that lexical matching can benefit from increased topical alignment; yet (ii) verification degrades (higher EER), consistent with lexical similarity being a poor proxy for calibrated authorship decisions.
Importantly, TF-IDF remains substantially below strong neural baselines overall, suggesting that AuthBench is not dominated by trivial lexical overlap and remains robust and challenging under a diagnostic that would otherwise amplify topic shortcuts.

\subsection{Instructions Given to Participants}
\label{sec:checklist-d1}
This work does not involve human subjects experiments, crowdworker annotation, or participant-provided responses.
Therefore, there are no participant instructions to report.

\paragraph{Human-in-the-loop quality checks.}
Although we do not use participants, we include a limited human-in-the-loop review for dataset quality and correctness as part of the construction pipeline.
Reviewers inspected samples of normalized instances and slice distributions to (i) validate field mappings (language/genre/source), (ii) spot-check filtering behavior (e.g., obvious boilerplate, malformed text), and (iii) verify that anonymization and deduplication behaved as intended.
This review is a research quality assurance step rather than a human-subject study and does not involve collecting new personal data.
